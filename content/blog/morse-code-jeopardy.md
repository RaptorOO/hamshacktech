If you practice CW with a group on Zoom, you've been here. The canned QSO:
name, QTH, rig, antenna, weather, 73. Then "tell us what you're having for
dinner." Then "send us your favorite car ever." And on and on it goes. None
of these are bad exercises. The problem is that we've all done every one of
them before, many, many times, and it shows.

Then comes the moment every group knows too well. The host unmutes and asks,
"So... what should we practice?"

Silence. A dozen video squares, and not one idea among them.

After enough of those silences, I decided our sessions needed something new.
So I built a fun tool for our Zoom practice: **[Morse Code
Jeopardy](/cw-tools/morse-code-jeopardy/)**, a free, browser-based game show
for CW groups on Zoom. It's now live on the [CW Tools](/cw-tools/) page.

![Morse Code Jeopardy mid-game: some tiles solved, one Daily Double showing, and a Q-Signals tile in play](/img/blog/morse-code-jeopardy/01-morse-code-jeopardy-board.png)

## How a round works

The board looks just like the TV show: six categories across the top, five
tiles in each column from $200 to $1,000. The twist is that each row is a
different speed. The $200 tiles play at 6 WPM, and they climb from there
through 9, 12 and 16 WPM, all the way to 20 WPM for the $1,000 tiles.

1. A player calls out a category and a dollar amount, just like on TV.
2. The host clicks the tile. After a three-second "stand by" countdown, the
   hidden answer plays in Morse.
3. If the group copies it, the host clicks **Got it** and the tile's full value
   goes into the Group Score.
4. If the group can't copy it, the host clicks **Play Slower**. It replays at
   about 70% speed, and then 50% if needed. Each replay is still worth
   something: half the tile's value on the second play and a quarter on the
   third.
5. Still stumped? **Reveal** shows the answer, and clicking a revealed tile
   plays it again so everyone can hear what they missed.

The categories are Numbers, Callsigns, Names, QTHs, Q-Signals and Ham Talk.
They're full of the things you'll actually hear on the air: RST reports,
POTA references, grid squares, real-world callsign formats, and a few
answers that are there just to get a laugh when the group finally copies
them. ("QRP 1500 WATTS" comes to mind.) Yes, that's my own call on the board
in the screenshot. It's in the Callsigns list, so you might get to copy me
too.

## The Daily Double: your turn on the key

Every column hides one **Daily Double**. When a player finds one, the roles
flip: that player has to think of an answer that fits the category and
**send it live on their own key** while the rest of the group copies. The
tile is scored like any other. It's worth full value if the group gets it on
the first send, and less if the sender has to repeat it.

This turned out to be my favorite part. Receiving is only half of CW, and a
low-pressure moment of sending to friends is great practice for the half
that happens when you're the one on the key.

## Nobody loses

This is the design decision I care about most: **there are no individual
scores.** The whole group builds one shared Group Score together.

Our study group runs from brand-new beginners to folks copying comfortably at
20 WPM. The moment you put individual scores on a board, the faster copiers
win every round, and the people who most need the practice start to dread it.
With one shared score, a beginner who nails a $200 tile at 6 WPM is putting
real points on the board for the team. The 20 WPM tiles are still there for
anyone who wants a stretch, but nobody is being ranked.

## A little science: why 6 WPM still sounds like real CW

The game uses **Farnsworth timing**. Every character is sent at a crisp 25 WPM
character speed (adjustable), so an "R" always sounds like an R does on the
air. The slower tile speeds come from stretching the *gaps between* characters
and words, not from slowing the characters themselves.

It matters because your ear learns each character as a single sound pattern,
a rhythm, and not as a string of dits and dahs to count. Slow the characters
way down and they turn into something to count. Keep them at speed and add
breathing room between them, and you're training your ear on the same sound
you'll hear in a real QSO. That's the approach programs like CW Academy use
for the same reason.

## Hosting it on Zoom

The game is built to be run by one host and screen-shared to the group, so
it's designed for a desktop or laptop browser, not a phone. To host:

1. Open [Morse Code Jeopardy](/cw-tools/morse-code-jeopardy/) in a browser
   window.
2. In Zoom, click **Share Screen** and pick just that browser window.
3. Turn on **Share Sound** before you click Share. **If you skip this step,
   nobody else will hear the code.** Ask me how I know.
4. Adjust the character speed, tone, volume and the five tile speeds if you
   like, then click **Let's Play**.

When the board is cleared, the game shows your final Group Score. Screenshot
it and brag to your group.
