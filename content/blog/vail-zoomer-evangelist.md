HamShackTech is mostly about bringing modern technology into the ham
shack — remote operation, radio mods, Stream Deck automation, that
kind of thing. But there's a second obsession running underneath all
of it right now: I'm learning CW, and I am nowhere near done.

I'm currently in my second semester of [CW
Academy](https://cwa.cwops.org/), the free, volunteer-run course
CWops offers to help hams learn and improve Morse code. If you're
thinking about learning CW yourself, I can't recommend it enough —
but that's a post for another day.

What this post is actually about is something I noticed a few weeks
into my first semester: an enormous amount of class time — real,
paid-in-attention class time — gets eaten alive by one dumb, solvable
problem. Getting clean, intelligible Morse code into a Zoom call is
harder than it has any right to be. A radio's sidetone through a
laptop mic picks up every fan, dog bark, and keyboard click in the
room, and the volume is usually too quiet for anyone else on the call
to copy comfortably. Multiply that across a class full of students all
fighting the same fight, and you can lose ten minutes of a session
just to "can you send that again, I couldn't quite get it."

Early in my own CW journey I stumbled onto the [Vail
Adapter](https://vailadapter.com/) — a nifty little USB device that
turns your paddle or straight key into a clean digital keying signal.

![The Vail Adapter website](/img/guides/vail-zoomer/01-vail-adapter.png)

Not long after, I found its companion app, [Vail
Zoomer](https://vailadapter.com/zoomer) — free, open-source software
that takes that signal and injects your sidetone directly into your
Zoom (or Teams, Discord, Meet) audio, right alongside your voice. No
microphone required to carry the code at all.

The difference was immediate and honestly a little embarrassing for
everyone else on the call. My dits and dahs came through crisp, at a
consistent volume, with zero room noise — while classmates were still
straining through mic static two rooms over. Within a few sessions,
students and even our advisors started asking the same question: "why
does your code sound so much better than everyone else's?"

I answered that question enough times in breakout rooms that I
basically became the class's unofficial Vail Zoomer evangelist. At
some point it stopped making sense to keep explaining the same audio
driver settings and Zoom microphone quirks one Zoom call at a time, so
I put together a proper visual, step-by-step tutorial instead —
screenshots and all, covering the setup wizard, the virtual audio
routing, and the handful of Zoom settings that have to be flipped or
Zoom will "helpfully" filter your CW tone out as background noise.

If you're a CW Academy student — or in any code class that meets over
video — and you want your sidetone to sound like it belongs on the
call instead of fighting to be heard on it, here's the full
